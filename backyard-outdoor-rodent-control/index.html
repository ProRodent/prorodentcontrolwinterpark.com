The yard is where the story starts.

Rodents rarely wander indoors by accident. They follow a trail that begins with food, water or cover in the yard and ends at a gap in the house. If the yard is attractive, the house becomes the next logical stop.

Central Florida yards offer a lot. Warm weather keeps plants producing for most of the year, lakes and canals supply water, and mature landscaping offers cover. None of that is wrong, but it explains why outdoor work matters.

What a technician looks for outside.

  • Burrows along fences, sheds, patios and the edge of the slab.
  • Dense groundcover or shrubs pressed against the walls.
  • Woodpiles, lumber or debris stacked on the ground.
  • Fruit, nuts, seed or pet food left out overnight.
  • Compost bins without tight lids.
  • Limbs, wires or vines that bridge to the roof.

A quick drawing of the yard goes into the notes, with the highest-traffic spots marked. That drawing guides both treatment and the changes we suggest.

Outdoor work is paired with sealing because a clean yard still needs a tight house, and with rat control when the signs point to roof rats.

Seasons, storms and the rainy months.

Heavy summer rain floods burrows and sends animals searching for dry ground. After a storm, walk the yard for new holes, washed-out soil and debris against the house. If a limb fell or a fence panel shifted, note it, because those changes create new routes.

Gardens, lakeshores and wooded edges.

Winter Park has public gardens such as Mead Botanical Garden and Kraft Azalea Garden, and plenty of lots that border water or wooded land. We do not claim anything about those parks. The practical point is that a lot touching a lake edge, a planted border or a neighbor's overgrown yard has more cover near the house than a lot with open lawn. A realistic weekend plan.

Spend one morning clearing fallen fruit, trimming limbs that touch the roof and moving the woodpile. Spend another sealing food containers and checking fence lines. Phone the desk for burrows, outdoor stations and any gap you cannot reach safely.

  • Do not hack back the vines and shrubs hugging the wall before we have looked at them..
  • Keep a flashlight within reach for the dark corners..

Before you call, a cleanup primer.

Calm, deliberate movements keep fine grit from drifting into the air you breathe.

  • Pellets that look harmless on a shelf release grit when scuffed, which is why dry cleanup is discouraged.
  • A shut-in space needs time to clear, so open it, leave and return when about thirty minutes have passed.
  • Bare skin should never meet pellets, urine spots or nesting scraps, so gloves go on first.
  • Wipe up wet waste with paper towels and never dry cloth, and keep soaking until nothing looks dusty.
  • Throw the bag away with ordinary garbage and rinse any reusable tools you handled.
  • Tell your doctor about any rodent contact if you fall ill within a few weeks, since we stick to pest control.

Yard questions people ask.

Do I need to cut down my trees?

Rarely. Trimming limbs that touch the roof is usually enough, and mature trees are one of the things people like about the area.

Will fruit trees draw rats?

Fallen fruit does. Picking early and clearing the ground each week removes a food source without removing the tree.

Are bird feeders a problem?

They can be, because spilled seed feeds rodents after dark. A tray beneath the feeder and a nightly sweep reduce the issue.

Is a compost pile okay?

A closed bin with a tight lid is fine. Open piles with food scraps are a steady meal and a hiding place.

Can you treat a neighbor's yard?

We can only work on the property of the person who books. We can describe what we see on the boundary so you can talk with the neighbor.
