Reading a Winter Park property.

Winter Park sits in Orange County among a scatter of lakes, and its older streets are often shaded by live oaks draped in Spanish moss, with palms planted beside them. That canopy is a pleasure to live under. For a rodent it can also be a highway, because a limb that touches a roof carries an animal from branch to eave without touching the ground.

Warm, humid weather finishes the picture. There is no real winter shutdown here, so rodents stay active across the calendar, and summer thunderstorms flood burrows and push animals toward dry shelter. The useful question is rarely whether rodents live nearby, since some surely do, but whether your building offers them a way in.

Mouse, roof rat or Norway rat?

The size of the doorway tells part of the story. Mice pass through openings near a pencil's width, and a rat wants about a half inch. The animal's route tells the rest: roof rats prefer wires, fence rails and palm trunks, while Norway rats prefer the ground and dig beside slabs, sheds and footings.

The trap, the metal and the cleanup all change with the species. So we name the animal before quoting a price, starting from your phone description and confirming it on the lot.

Rainy-season habits.

From early summer into autumn the afternoons turn stormy, and now and then a tropical system passes through. Flooded burrows drive their owners upward and indoors. When the sky clears, take a slow lap of the house and look for new holes, a vent cover knocked askew and any limb or frond that landed on the roof.

  • Before the wet months arrive, wiggle every vent cover and pipe collar, then look over the lower corners of the garage door.
  • Move pet food, feeder mix and grass seed into bins that latch.
  • Set firewood and spare boards on a rack a few feet from any wall.
  • Prune fronds and branches that reach the roofline or siding.

Steps to take before we get there.

Three habits protect your household and keep the clues intact. Leave droppings untouched, keep little ones and animals out of that room, and let any trap you set stay put.

  • Photograph pellets and tooth marks beside a coin so the size is clear.
  • Record the room and the clock time of each noise.
  • Close the door on the room with the most activity.
  • Leave loose bait and glue boards on the shelf, since both bring hazards of their own.

How a visit unfolds.

Maybe the problem is one mouse in the pantry, or maybe it is a nest under a truck hood. Either way, the job moves through four stages, always in this order.

  1. Survey.

    The first stop is wherever the evidence is freshest, be it the attic, garage, crawl space or yard. From there we circle the roofline, hunting for fresh droppings and for the openings animals are actually using.

  2. Trap.

    Stations are placed along the runs the clues reveal, chosen for the species at hand and tucked where kids and pets cannot reach.

  3. Seal.

    Cracks, vents and gnawed openings are covered with flashing, hardware cloth or a sealant tough enough to resist teeth.

  4. Recheck.

    On the return trip we clear the traps, tug on each seal and take up whatever cleanup or repair work is still outstanding.

How to tell rodents are in the building.

Two or more of these in one corner usually point to this week's visitors and not last year's leftovers.

  • Pellets.

    Tiny dark pellets under the sink, behind the fridge or scattered across attic insulation.

  • Gnawing.

    Fresh tooth marks look pale and rough, and you may find them on rafters, plastic pipe, cable jackets or the corner of a cardboard carton.

  • Night noises.

    Scratching, tiny footsteps or a soft thump behind drywall or overhead, usually after everyone has gone to bed.

  • Nest material.

    A heap of torn paper, pulled insulation, scraps of cloth or dried palm fiber stuffed into a protected nook.

  • Grease trails.

    Dark, oily streaks along walls, pipes and joists mark a lane that some animal runs every night.

  • Odor.

    A sharp ammonia smell in a shut room, or a heavy, sweetish rot where an animal has died.

Spot two or more? Phone before the damage grows.

Landmarks callers mention, and why we still ask for a street.

Charles Hosmer Morse Museum of American Art.

The museum was founded in 1942 and has been at 445 North Park Avenue since 1995. Callers sometimes use it to describe where they live or work. The name orients us on a map, yet the rodent plan depends on your building, so we book each inspection by street address and ask about the delivery door, shared walls and trash area.

Casa Feliz.

Casa Feliz was built in 1933 and moved about 900 feet to its present site. It is a handy reminder that buildings get altered, and an altered house has seams where old and new materials meet. If your own house has been added onto, say so on the call, because we inspect those joints first.

Kraft Azalea Garden.

This public park at 1363 Alabama Drive sits on Lake Maitland, and it was developed in 1938. If your lot borders open water or a planted edge, tell us. Shelter close to the house shortens an animal's trip, and it changes where we set the first stations.

Rollins College.

Rollins College opened for classes in 1885 and is one of the first places many people name when giving directions. Wherever rentals and shared walls exist, a rodent can travel between units, so we ask whether your unit shares a wall, a ceiling or a trash enclosure with a neighbor.

Mead Botanical Garden.

According to Wikipedia, the garden takes in several ecosystems, with an amphitheater, a butterfly garden and a discovery barn. We claim nothing more about it. For a nearby home, the practical question is whether a wooded border or leaf litter touches your property line.

Why the surroundings shape the plan.

A landmark helps us read a map, though it says nothing about the animals in your walls. A dead-end lane, a lakefront lot and a shop next to a loading dock each need a different first step.

Vehicles count as buildings too. A pickup, camper, trailer or golf cart that has sat for weeks may hold a nest, so lift the hood and sniff the cabin vents when you return. One gnawed wire caught early costs less than a new harness.

A town with a long timeline.

Winter Park was incorporated as a town in 1887 and as a city in 1925. For a homeowner, that long history matters in one narrow way: many properties have been renovated more than once, and each renovation leaves seams. Tell the dispatcher which parts of the house are original and which were added, and we inspect the seams first.

Houses left empty for months.

Part-time residents leave houses shut for long stretches, and a still, dark house suits rodents. On the way out, finish opened food, close up gaps at the garage door and arrange for someone to peek inside now and then. Back home, check the attic hatch before unpacking.

Questions people raise before booking.

How can I tell mice from rats?

Droppings help: mouse pellets run small, about like a grain of rice, while rat pellets are noticeably bigger. Habits help too, since rats often work overhead or dig outside and mice favor kitchens and pantries.

How do rodents get into homes in Winter Park?

Through the same gaps as anywhere: vents, soffits, door sweeps, pipe sleeves and roof edges. Limbs that touch the roof add an overhead route, and humid weather keeps animals active all year.

Will traps alone fix the problem?

Traps remove the animals inside, but sealing stops new ones from arriving. We do both, in that order, and check back to confirm.

Are the treatments safe near kids and pets?

Mention every child and pet when you call. Stations are enclosed or placed out of reach, and before leaving the technician tells you what went where.

Can someone come today?

The desk is staffed all day and all night, and a same-day slot is offered whenever the calendar has one open. Ask for the earliest.

What comes after the first appointment?

We return to empty traps, test the seals and advise on cleanup or repair, on dates you are given at the start.
