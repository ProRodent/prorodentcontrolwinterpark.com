Heat, storms and shifting burrows.

Hot afternoons push animals into shade, and shade often means attics, garages and the dark space under a deck. A cooler house, ironically, attracts them even more.

Older lots and newer additions.

Where a long-settled place has older houses and newer ones side by side, the older structures often carry more seams from past repairs. We do not claim that about any one house. We ask for the age of each part and look at the joints first.

Trees matter in older lots. A limb that has grown against a roof over many years can bend flashing or loosen a vent cover, so we check the roof edge on every visit.

Before you call, a few rules to protect yourself.

Rodent waste can carry germs, so careful cleanup is slow, damp and quiet. Each step traces to the CDC pages named at the foot of this article.

  • Skip the broom and the shop vacuum. Dry droppings crumble, and anything that stirs them sends fine particles into your breathing space.
  • A shut-in space needs time to clear, so open it, leave and return when about thirty minutes have passed.
  • Bare skin should never meet pellets, urine spots or nesting scraps, so gloves go on first.
  • Spray droppings and urine spots with disinfectant, or with bleach cut with water, until they glisten, then allow roughly five minutes.
  • Dead animals stay off bare hands, and a gloved hand or a shovel moves them into the bag.
  • Feeling sick after handling rodent waste is a matter for a physician, so tell them exactly what you touched.

Agency references.

Federal agencies wrote the rules on cleanup and on handling bait, and those pages are linked here.

  • Rodent cleanup page at cdc.gov: How to deal with urine stains, droppings and chewed bedding without stirring up dust.
  • CDC page: prevent hantavirus exposure: Shut the entrances first, and only then deal gently with old droppings and nests.
