Guessing is the expensive way.

Plenty of homeowners start with a trap from the hardware store and a tube of foam. Sometimes that works for a week. Often it covers one hole while three others stay open, and the animal that left a pellet in the pantry was never a mouse at all.

An inspection reverses the order. Evidence and entry points come first, a price comes second and any trap comes last. You spend an hour finding out what is true before you spend a dollar on what to do about it.

The route a technician walks.

The walk-through starts outside, because most rodent routes are visible from the ground. The technician circles the foundation, scans the roofline from a ladder or from the ground, and checks every place a pipe, wire or vent passes through the shell of the building.

  • Perimeter: foundation vents, utility penetrations, door sweeps and the seam where siding meets the slab.
  • Roof edge: soffits, fascia returns, ridge vents and limbs that touch the shingles.
  • Attic or crawl space: insulation disturbance, droppings, nests and gnawed wiring.
  • Interior hotspots: under sinks, behind appliances, inside the water heater closet and along garage walls.

Photographs are taken as the visit goes along, so the written estimate can point at the exact spot and not just describe it.

How the estimate is built.

The estimate separates work into steps that can stand alone. Up to four lines can appear, covering trapping, sealing, sanitizing and repair, and each is priced alone because not every household needs all four. If an attic is clean and only a vent cover is broken, the estimate says so.

Getting the house ready for the visit.

A little preparation shortens the walk-through. Clear the path to the attic hatch, open the garage and shed, and move pet food off the floor. If you noticed droppings, leave them where they are. Take a photo with a coin beside them to show size, and write down the room and the time of day.

Ahead of the technician.

  • Park a vehicle with debris under the hood until someone has checked it..
  • Carry grass seed, dog food and feeder mix indoors until this is sorted..
  • Bag any gnawed item and save it for the visit..
  • Locate the breaker box, in case a gnawed cable needs shutting off..

Before you call: federal health guidance in short form.

The agencies largely agree on how to clean up safely, so their shared method is boiled down here.

  • Whatever you reach for first, put it back if it is dry: brooms, vacuums, compressed air and rags all count.
  • Let a cross-breeze pass through for roughly half an hour, then go in, and skip the sniff test.
  • Bare skin should never meet pellets, urine spots or nesting scraps, so gloves go on first.
  • Disinfect the surface under and around the waste too, since urine travels beyond the visible spot.
  • Throw the bag away with ordinary garbage and rinse any reusable tools you handled.
  • Feeling sick after handling rodent waste is a matter for a physician, so tell them exactly what you touched.

Inspection questions people ask first.

What does the inspector actually look at?

The outside perimeter, the roofline, vents and soffits, the attic or crawl space, the garage, and the kitchen and utility areas where signs tend to cluster.

Do I have to be home?

You or an adult you trust should let the technician in and be reachable by phone. Pets should be secured before the visit starts.

Will the estimate lock me into the work?

No. The estimate is a written list with a price on each line, and you decide which lines to approve.

How long does a walk-through take?

Smaller houses often take under an hour, while large lots, multiple outbuildings or a heavy infestation stretch it. The dispatcher can give a rough window when you book.

Can an inspection find nothing?

Sometimes the noise turns out to be a branch, a pipe expanding or a squirrel. We say so plainly and explain how we ruled rodents out.
