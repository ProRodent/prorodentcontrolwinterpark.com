Why placement beats quantity.

Twenty traps scattered across an attic catch less than six traps set on the exact runs. Rodents travel the same paths night after night, hugging edges and following beams and wires. A trap on that line gets investigated, and a trap three feet off it gets ignored.

Reading the lines takes a flashlight and patience. Dark grease smears show where fur and skin oils touch a surface. Droppings cluster along the route and thicken near the nest. Gnaw marks, dragged insulation and tiny footprints in dust fill in the rest.

Every device choice is explained during the visit. If bait is ever part of the plan, it goes only inside tamper-resistant stations, and the EPA page in the sources list says why store-bought products come packaged that way.

Visits, checks and the log.

The first visit sets stations and records each location on paper or a phone log. Return visits empty and reset them, move any that stayed quiet and add new ones where fresh signs have appeared. A quiet station is information, not failure, because it tells us a route is dead.

  • Visit one: place stations, photograph the starting evidence and note every location.
  • Return checks: collect catches, re-bait or reset and move quiet stations.
  • Final check: confirm that no new signs have appeared and that the seals hold.

Trapping alone does not stop the next animal. Our sealing work closes the gaps once the lanes are known, and the inspection explains how the whole plan is priced.

Mention any leaf mound, firewood stack or shed near the back fence when you call, since stations often start there.

What you can do between visits.

Keep pet bowls and bird feeders out of reach after dark, and tell household members not to move or touch stations. If a station seems disturbed, leave it and phone the desk. Opening one yourself can expose you to droppings and releases dust you should not breathe.

Ahead of the technician.

  • Keep vacuums and brooms well away from droppings..
  • Hold the bleach until the whole problem has been mapped..
  • Set a quarter beside each gnaw mark before the photo, which makes the size obvious..
  • Work out roughly how many weeks ago the signs started..

Before you call, a cleanup primer.

Following these steps takes no phone call, since they mirror the advice agencies give to anyone who spots droppings indoors.

  • Whatever you reach for first, put it back if it is dry: brooms, vacuums, compressed air and rags all count.
  • Garages, sheds, attics and spare rooms that stayed shut tend to hold stale air, so open them and wait about thirty minutes.
  • Gloves protect the hands, and hands touch faces, so keep the gloves on until the last bag is tied.
  • Spray droppings and urine spots with disinfectant, or with bleach cut with water, until they glisten, then allow roughly five minutes.
  • Dead animals stay off bare hands, and a gloved hand or a shovel moves them into the bag.
  • A lingering fever or shortness of breath after exposure deserves a physician's attention.

Each safety remark rests on federal guidance, and a line beside every link says what it covers.

Trapping questions we hear.

Snap traps or bait stations: which do you use?

It depends on the room, the animal and who lives in the house. Enclosed snap stations suit most homes, and we explain any bait option before it goes anywhere.

Are glue boards part of the work?

We avoid them. They hold lizards, birds and pets as readily as rodents, and a struggling animal is distressing to find.

How many visits will it take?

Light cases may need one return, while an established colony takes longer. The schedule is stated at the start and adjusted as the log fills in.

Who removes the dead animals?

Technicians collect catches on each visit. Between visits, enclosed stations keep them out of sight.

Can I keep my own traps going?

Please ask first. Stray traps can scare animals off the lanes we are watching and muddy the log.
