Night Noises in the Ceiling: Winter Park, FL

Something is moving above the bedroom at midnight. This article helps you work out what you are hearing, record the clues that matter and decide whether the next step is a ladder or a phone call.

The house goes quiet, the air conditioner cycles off, and then it begins: a light scrabbling above the ceiling that stops when you hold your breath. Most people lie awake working out what it could be. This guide gives that guesswork some structure.

Describe the sound before you chase it.

A technician can narrow down the animal from a good description alone, so write yours down. Note when the noise starts, how long it lasts, whether it travels or stays put and how heavy it sounds.

What you hear.What it often suggests.Other possibilities.
Quick light patter, stopping and starting.Mice.Insects in the wall, ducts expanding.
Heavier running or thumping along a line.Rats.Squirrels in daytime, a loose duct.
Gnawing or rasping.Rodents chewing wood or plastic.Beetles in wood, a settling joist.
Rolling or dragging.A rat moving nest material or food.Something shifting in a stored box.
Squeaks or chirps.Young rodents in a nest.Birds in a soffit or vent.

None of these is a confirmed diagnosis. They are starting points, and the evidence that follows will confirm or rule them out.

Collect corroborating evidence.

Sound alone is weak evidence. Look for a second clue before you decide, and a third before you spend money.

  • Pellets: dark, rice-sized for mice, larger and capsule-shaped for rats.
  • Gnaw marks on wood, wire jackets, plastic pipe or food packaging.
  • Smudged runways along beams, pipes or the top of a wall.
  • Shredded insulation, paper or fabric gathered in a corner.
  • A stale, musky smell in the attic or a closet below it.

If you can safely reach the attic hatch, shine a flashlight along the edges and take photographs. Do not touch or sweep what you see.

Other things that make noise up there.

Not every sound is an animal. Air conditioning ducts expand and contract as they heat and cool, and the ticking can echo through the house. Roof boards flex with temperature. Tree limbs scrape on shingles in wind.

Squirrels run on the roof in daylight and are heavy-footed. Birds nest in vents during spring and early summer. Bats are protected in many places, and a noise accompanied by droppings under a vent should be handled by a qualified professional, since the rules around removal differ from those for rodents.

Snap the pellets or the bite marks, then say so when you call, and the visit gets quicker.

A listening log for three nights.

Keep a simple record for three nights. It takes a minute each evening and gives the technician something precise to work with.

  • Write the time you first hear the sound and the time it stops.
  • Note the room it seems to be above or beside.
  • Say whether the sound moves along the ceiling or stays in one place.
  • Record any weather, wind or air conditioner cycles at the same moment.
  • Photograph anything you find on the floor, in closets or around vents.

A noise that repeats at the same hour in the same spot usually points to an animal with a routine. Random sounds scattered across the night more often suggest a building noise.

Why Florida attics are different.

Attics here get hot, often very hot by afternoon, and animals tend to be most active after dark when temperatures fall. That can make the problem seem to appear suddenly at night, when it has been underway for days.

The heat also affects how you investigate. Go up early in the morning if at all, bring water, wear a mask and gloves and never step off the joists. If the hatch is stiff or the space is cramped, let a professional handle it.

Closing the way in.

Noises stop when animals leave or are removed and the entrances are shut. Trapping without sealing invites a replacement, and sealing without trapping can lock animals inside. The order matters: first locate the route, then trap, then close it.

Our trapping page explains how stations are placed, and the exclusion page lists the metals we use at gaps. Cleanup of nests and droppings comes afterward, described on the attic cleanup page.

What to do tonight.

If you hear noise right now, do not bang on the ceiling or pour anything into the attic. Noise can scatter animals into the walls. Instead, make a note of the time and location, turn off any lights near the hatch and keep pets away from that room.

Store food in sealed containers and take out the trash. Those small steps will not solve the problem, but they take away easy meals while you arrange a visit.

When the call is warranted.

Phone when you have two or more signs, when noise returns several nights in a row or when you notice a smell, stain or droppings. The dispatcher will ask for your listening log and any photographs, and an inspection will identify the animal and the path it uses.

Put the broom down before dialing.

Thinking of tidying up before our crew arrives? Work through this list in order and stop at any point that feels unclear.

  • Even a gentle swish of a dust mop can lift old pellets into the air, so no dry tool should start the job.
  • Give a closed-up space a half hour of fresh air before you step inside to work, with doors open, windows open and people elsewhere.
  • Treat each soiled surface as contaminated, wear gloves and keep gloved hands away from your mouth and eyes.
  • Spray droppings and urine spots with disinfectant, or with bleach cut with water, until they glisten, then allow roughly five minutes.
  • A second bag around the first adds insurance if the plastic is thin or the load is heavy.
  • Whether symptoms connect to a cleanup is a question for a health professional, so find one if you feel unwell.

Loose ends.

Is scratching always a rodent?

No. Squirrels, birds, cooling ducts and settling lumber can all make noise. The timing, location and repetition of the sound are the best clues.

Is it wise to climb up and look myself?

Only when the hatch is sound, the heat is bearable and you can wear a mask and gloves. Florida attics get very hot, and stepping between joists is risky.

Will a noise stop on its own?

Rarely. Animals that find shelter and food tend to stay and breed, so a quiet week does not prove the problem is gone.

What time of night should I listen?

Rats and mice are most active in the first hours after dark and again before dawn. Listening then gives the most reliable picture.

Do I need to know the animal before I call?

No. Describe the sound, the location and when it happens, and the technician will identify the animal during an inspection.
