Rodenticides, Pets and Wildlife: What to Know in Winter Park

Poison seems like the easy answer to a rodent problem. This article explains the risks to dogs, cats and wildlife, what the EPA has said about product restrictions and the alternatives that avoid them.

A cluttered garage, a family dog and a scratching sound in the attic can make a box of bait look like a tidy solution. It is worth knowing why many professionals favor other methods first.

The risks nobody mentions at the store.

Rodenticides are designed to kill mammals. Dogs, cats, kids and wild animals can be hurt by eating the bait itself, or by eating a rodent that already fed on it.

  • Dogs and cats may eat bait or find a poisoned animal.
  • Birds of prey and other wildlife can be affected by eating poisoned rodents.
  • Children can be exposed if bait is left in reachable places.
  • Animals that die inside walls create odors and fly problems.

If you suspect that a pet has eaten rodenticide, ring a veterinarian or an animal poison hotline at once. Waiting for symptoms costs precious time.

What the EPA says.

To cut down on accidental poisonings, the EPA has put limits on certain rodenticide products. The rules touch what shoppers may buy, how products are packaged and how bait is placed. The details can change, so we point to the agency page linked below and not to a summary here.

If you own bait already, read its label carefully. Labels are legally binding instructions, and they spell out where it can and cannot be used.

Dead animals in the walls.

Poison does not always kill quickly, and the animal may die in a place you cannot reach. Odor can linger for days and flies sometimes show up. Our odor control page describes how we locate and remove them.

Protecting wildlife.

Central Florida has owls, hawks, foxes, bobcats and many other animals that eat rodents. A poisoned rodent can pass its toxin along. Choosing trapping and sealing over loose bait is one practical way to keep that from happening.

  • Keep bait, if you use it, inside tamper-resistant stations as the label says.
  • Never scatter bait loose.
  • Dispose of leftover product as directed by the label and local rules.
  • Report any suspected poisoning of wildlife to local authorities.

Pet-safe habits around the house.

  • Feed pets indoors or pick up bowls at night.
  • Keep trash and compost in lidded bins.
  • Store pet food in sealed containers.
  • Clean up fallen fruit and spilled seed.
  • Block pets from garages and attics where traps or bait may be present.

Tell the technician about every pet and any child who visits. The plan will account for them, and traps will be placed where they cannot be reached.

If you have already used bait.

Tell us. We need to know where it was placed, what product it was and whether any animals might have fed on it. That affects safe handling and the order of work.

Remove leftover bait according to the label, and record where you found any dead animals. Wear gloves, and use the cleanup steps in our cleaning guide.

Asking about safer options.

Call and ask how we would handle your property without relying on poison. We will explain which traps suit your animal, how stations are placed and what the follow-up visits involve.

Before you call: federal health guidance in short form.

Rodent waste can carry germs, so careful cleanup is slow, damp and quiet. Each step traces to the CDC pages named at the foot of this article.

  • Leave powdery debris undisturbed until it is damp, because the CDC steers people away from sweeping and vacuuming for good reason.
  • Fresh air does the first part of the job: half an hour with the openings wide and nobody in the room.
  • Peel the gloves off inside out when you are done, bag them if they are disposable and scrub with soap and water.
  • Spray generously, step back, give it a few minutes and then lift the mess with disposable towels.
  • Throw the bag away with ordinary garbage and rinse any reusable tools you handled.
  • Tell your doctor about any rodent contact if you fall ill within a few weeks, since we stick to pest control.

Short answers.

Is rodenticide illegal?

Not categorically, but the EPA restricts certain products and how they are sold and used. The EPA page linked below has current details.

Can my dog get sick from a dead rodent?

Animals that eat poisoned rodents can be affected. If you think a pet has been exposed, contact a veterinarian right away.

Why does poison sometimes cause smells?

Animals that die inside walls are not removed, and the body decays. That is a common reason for odor calls.

What do you use instead?

Where possible, we rely on trapping, exclusion and sanitation. If bait is ever warranted, it is used according to label rules in secured stations.

Can I use bait myself?

Read the label and the EPA page first. Consumer products have restrictions, and misuse can harm pets, children and wildlife.
